Why a used-rate is not a result

Thalamus records, for every retrieval, which memory nodes it put in front of the agent — and then judges whether the agent used them, by matching the node's distinctive terms against what the agent said and did next. That judgement is cheap, runs after every session, and produces the number the project has been quoting: 63.3% of retrieved nodes used.

The problem is that a coding session and the memory it retrieves are about the same subject. Term overlap is guaranteed before any retrieval happens. So the question is not what the rate is, but how much of it survives when the retrieval is deliberately mismatched to the work.

The control is a permutation. Re-judge every retrieval against a different session's output window and see what the judge still calls used. Whatever it scores there is the floor: vocabulary the sessions share by being the same project. Two constraints make it a fair floor — the partner is always a different session, and it is drawn from the same window-length stratum, because the judge's used-rate moves with window length alone and an unstratified rotation would measure that instead.

The estimator

κ = (p − p̄₀) / (1 − p̄₀): the share of the available headroom the judge captures. p is the measured rate, p̄₀ the mean over 200 rotations. A judge that scores 63% where chance scores 57% is not 63% right — it is 14.0% of the way from chance to perfect.

The pre-registered hypothesis is falsified

The hypothesis was that tool-call inputs would discriminate better than prose, because acting on a retrieved path is a narrower coincidence than repeating a word. It does not: κ moves -0.002, from 0.140 shipped to 0.138, well inside the null's own half-width of 0.024.

The prose-only judge is the informative half of the comparison: it scores κ=0.053, clearly worse than either. So prose and tool calls are not two channels of different quality — the shipped judge is stronger than either alone because it sees more text, which is the same length effect that shows up in every other cut of this corpus. The axis is quantity of window, not kind of evidence.

Window length is the confound the strata exist to hold: the median window in this corpus is 121,656 characters and the longest is 367,623. Term membership is tested anywhere in that window, so a longer session mechanically scores more used — the judge partly reports how long the operator kept working.

Read κ figures with their null's design attached

The two designs differ by 0.046 in κ — larger than the spread between the best and worst of the three judges that have any signal at all. A κ quoted without saying how its null was drawn is not comparable to another κ.

It was put to this experiment that the difference from the project's earlier hand-run figure (κ≈0.086) would be mostly this — the same instrument read against a looser yardstick. The measurement says otherwise, and in the opposite direction: dropping the stratum makes the null easier here (54.9% against 57.3%) and κ correspondingly higher (0.186), which moves away from 0.086 rather than toward it. So the null design is worth a lot, and it is not what explains the older number; the remaining candidates are corpus construction (that figure was computed over tap-reconstructed traces, this one over the graph's own Trace census) and a three-rotation null with no interval. Neither is worth a rerun: both figures' intervals are wide enough to contain each other.

The auditable subset: claims only

Not every verdict can be re-derived. Claim vertices are content-addressed on (kind, normalised description), so rewriting a claim mints a new vertex and the text behind a stored verdict is still the text that was judged. Thread and Session are upserted latest-wins: their titles, descriptions and summaries are overwritten in place, and ingested_at carries the writing session's timestamp rather than the write time, so nothing in the graph records that the text moved.

That splits the corpus into an auditable part and an exposed one. 1,785 of 2,445 verdicts (73.0%) are claims; the rest sit on text that may have changed underneath them.

κ intervals are paired: each bootstrap draw resamples sessions and recomputes the rate and its null together, because κ is a contrast.

The purge comparison, withdrawn

This was pre-registered as a control. It is not one.

On 2026-07-30 the graph lost 307 Session vertices that were never sessions — headless distillation subprocesses that had been firing the SessionEnd hook and distilling themselves, 69% of all sessions. The pre-registration treats "did κ move across the purge?" as a falsifier, on the reasoning that extraction self-talk is the most topic-matched prose in the corpus and would inflate a topic detector.

It could not have moved. Those sessions made no retrievals. Both snapshots yield the same 222 retrievals across the same 29 sessions; they contributed no output window to the rate and were never in the rotation pool. The only difference is 37 verdicts (1.49%) that pointed at purged nodes. Reporting the non-move as a passed control would imply a test that had a way to fail.

What this costs to fix

The gap between the real rate and the null is 6.0 points. To bound that gap to ±1 point you need enough paired verdicts to overcome how often a verdict flips under rotation — measured here at 24.5% — inflated by the clustering of verdicts inside sessions.

That is roughly 37,717 verdicts, or about 447 sessions at this corpus's 84 verdicts per session. The corpus today has 2,445 verdicts across 29 sessions.

More data will not rescue this instrument

An order of magnitude more sessions, at one operator's real working rate, is years. The conclusion is not "collect more" — it is that a lexical judge cannot be made precise enough at single-operator volume, and the next move is a better instrument: human-labelled ground truth to calibrate against, and a randomized withholding policy that makes the counterfactual internal to real work.

Threats to this result

Replay is 99.1% faithful, and fidelity is the wrong measure of the exposure

Re-judging from the pinned snapshot reproduces 2,423 of 2,445 verdicts that eval sync stored live. The 22 that differ are almost all memory_open_threads, and the stored evidence strings show why: the same node scored "8/29 terms" then and "7/22" now. The node's text changed.

The exposure is larger than the mismatch count suggests. Fidelity counts verdicts that flipped; text can change without flipping one. The right denominator is every verdict on a mutable node kind — Thread and Session, which are upserted latest-wins — and that is 660 of 2,445 verdicts (27.0%), not the 0.9% that visibly disagree. The bias is directional, too: a session that retrieves a thread and then rewrites that thread's description moves the real window's terms toward the node, and does not move any rotated partner's. That inflates the rate and not the null.

The claims-only cut above is the check that this did not produce the headline. The fix is to record the judged term-set (or its hash) on the RETURNS edge at judgement time, which makes a verdict a historical fact rather than a re-derivation.

The bounded judges have no headroom, and κ and the odds ratio disagree

Pre-registered as a condition that would make a result uninterpretable, and it fired: bounded-1 and bounded-3 score 1.3% and 1.9% used, against nulls near zero. A judge with almost no positives is being scored on almost no headroom, so its κ is unstable — read those rows as "no signal available", not as "no signal found".

The disagreement is the interesting part. On the odds-ratio scale the bounded judges order cleanly — 8.3× at one turn, 6.2× at three, 2.5× at ten, 1.3× unbounded — the opposite ranking to κ, which divides by a headroom that shrinks with the window. Neither column decides it. Measuring whether utility decays with distance needs a within-session shifted-window design, not a comparison of judges with different base rates.

Other limits, stated rather than implied:

  • One operator, one machine, one harness configuration. This is an observational result about a single deployment, not a benchmark.
  • The frame is blind to non-retrieval. A node that was never returned has no edge, so harm from failing to retrieve is structurally invisible here.
  • The null tests the judge, not the retrieval. Permutation can say how much of a verdict is vocabulary; it cannot say whether the retrieval changed what the agent did. That needs randomized withholding.
  • Ranker configurations are mixed. Most of this corpus predates the ranker ledger, so these rates straddle configuration changes.

What is deliberately absent

The graph itself is not published. It is one operator's session history and the evidence archive holds their transcripts verbatim — credentials, client names and personal history included. The snapshot hash lets anyone holding the same state verify that these numbers came from it; it does not let a stranger recompute them from scratch, and no wording here should imply otherwise.
